UPWARDLY — MAJOR OWNER DIRECTIVE

COMPLETE PLAYER-CAPABILITY, PHYSICS, SOLVER, AND PROCEDURAL-GENERATION VERIFICATION

DISCOVER THE ENTIRE MOVEMENT SYSTEM. VERIFY EVERY MECHANICALLY DISTINCT OUTCOME. ELIMINATE BLIND SPOTS.

Claude, I want to address a fundamental concern about Upwardly's procedural-generation and solvability-verification architecture.

We have invested substantial development time building a system intended to determine whether a human player can physically traverse a procedurally generated course.

Yet we are still discovering fundamental player abilities and traversal outcomes that the verification system does not consistently account for.

Recent examples include:

- A climber reaching a crumbling moving platform through a ledge catch rather than a conventional landing.
- Individually solvable route links failing when connected into a continuous sequence.
- Jump-pad trajectories intersecting hanging hazards one spiral revolution above.
- Hazards placed before a jump pad not being checked against that subsequently placed pad.
- Jump pads from neighboring slices being excluded from hazard-corridor checks.
- Forked routes being omitted from serialized debugging fixtures.
- Avalanche encounters passing one arrival-time proof but failing at another legitimate arrival time.
- Moving-platform and hazard interactions changing according to the player's preceding movement state.

These discoveries raise a serious architectural question:

DOES OUR SOLVER ACTUALLY UNDERSTAND THE COMPLETE MOVEMENT AND INTERACTION SYSTEM OF THE GAME IT IS SUPPOSED TO VERIFY?

I no longer want to discover fundamental omissions one failing seed at a time.

I want a comprehensive investigation of the entire system.

This is not a request to make the existing failing seeds pass and declare victory.

This is a request to establish, with evidence, what our verification system actually understands, what it does not understand, and how we will eliminate the gaps.

7. SOLVABILITY MUST BE CONTINUOUS

An individually possible jump does not prove a continuous course is possible.

The generator must not assume that successfully reaching a platform resets the player to an ideal condition.

Example:

A player completes Jump A with substantial horizontal velocity.

The next jump requires an immediate reversal on a narrow platform.

Jump B may be possible from a stationary centered position.

But if Jump A cannot realistically deliver the player into a state from which Jump B is achievable, the combined sequence may be impossible.

The solver must investigate that continuity.

This applies to:

- Momentum.
- Landing position.
- Vertical velocity.
- Movement lockouts.
- Moving-platform timing.
- Hazard timing.
- Enemy encounters.
- Temporary platform states.
- Cross-slice transitions.

Required behavior

The verification system must establish a continuous, physically valid route from the starting state to the destination.

It must not manufacture an ideal state between transitions unless the player can legitimately reach that state through actual game mechanics.

The route's state at the end of one transition must be compatible with the starting state of the next.

If the player can legitimately stop, reposition, wait, or reset momentum, the solver should recognize that.

If they cannot, the solver must not assume they can.

8. DO NOT CONFUSE PHYSICAL POSSIBILITY WITH GUARANTEED PLAYER SUCCESS

I want this distinction handled correctly.

A difficult section can be physically solvable even when most players will fail.

That is acceptable, especially in NGH.

We are not trying to guarantee success from every possible player action.

There is a difference between:

A. A legitimate but difficult route.

A skilled player can execute a valid sequence of movements.

B. An unrecoverable player mistake.

The player chooses an incorrect timing or movement and falls.

C. An unavoidable generator-created failure.

The course requires mutually incompatible movements or produces an unavoidable collision during otherwise valid traversal.

D. A solver limitation.

A valid route may exist, but the solver's current search cannot find or verify it.

These must be distinguished.

A solver timeout is not proof that the course is impossible.

A successful isolated jump is not proof that the whole course is possible.

A theoretical path requiring unavailable player controls is not a valid solution.
